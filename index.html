    Project A · UC Santa Cruz AIEA Lab

    Fault Recovery in RL Locomotion Through Online Residual Adaptation

    Can a small correction module, updated during execution, restore a quadruped's gait after a hardware fault without retraining its underlying policy?

    Nominal gait base policy · healthy 0.50 m/s step 000

    01 / 06

    Premise

    01 — Premise

    Most policies are trained on robots that never break.

    In the real world motors weaken, joints seize, and sensors start reporting values that aren't true. A locomotion policy trained only under ideal conditions has never once been shown what failure feels like — so it has no response to it.

    02 — Apparatus

    A quadruped designed to fail on purpose.

    Twelve revolute joints in PyBullet, physics at 240 Hz stepped down to 60 Hz control. PPO trained across ten seeds at 1.5M timesteps — all ten converged to a usable gait, so every one of them is in the experiment.

    03 — Injection

    At step 200, something fails.

    Five fault families on a matched seed set, so every fault type is compared against identical initial conditions. Three corrupt the physics — what the robot can do. Two corrupt the observations — what the robot knows.

    torque_limitjoint_lockactuation_delaysensor_dropoutsensor_noise

    04 — Method

    Correct around the fault, don't retrain through it.

    A twelve-number residual sits between the frozen base policy and the actuators. It integrates velocity-tracking error and spreads the correction across joints through an influence matrix measured on the healthy robot — no gradients, no network, no fault detector. The ablation is fixed versus re-identified online by recursive least squares: does learning the damaged dynamics buy anything over correcting with a model of the undamaged ones?

    Built, with the influence matrix fit on all ten seeds. Its first test was a negative result.

    Independent halves of the fit disagreed about how joint offsets move forward speed. Holding one offset for a whole episode showed why: the change saturates near 0.055 m/s however much is asked for, and the same holds on a damaged robot. The base policy tracks velocity in closed loop, so it cancels a posture offset. That is authority, not noise — more data would not fix it. The correction has to act on gait amplitude instead; that probe is written and not yet run.

    05 — Measurement

    First, build an instrument worth trusting.

    The original recovery criterion latched onto the first instantaneous velocity crossing and scored ~100% recovery on every fault — including 100% recovery alongside a 36.5% fall rate, which cannot both be true. It was rebuilt: velocity smoothed over a 30-step causal window, degradation has to be observed before recovery can be, the speed must hold for 30 consecutive steps, and a trial that falls is never scored as recovered.

    Every recovery number predating that change was discarded.

    Revised again on 22 September: degradation and recovery are now judged on 2-second windows, about six strides, and the lasting deficit is recorded separately from the worst transient dip. The Baseline A table predates that revision and is due a re-run.

    06 — Finding

    The faults separate cleanly.

    Unadapted recovery runs from 1.2% on actuation_delay to 82.3% on sensor_noise. Every actuation-side fault is less recoverable than both sensor-side faults, with no overlap — which makes the physics/sensor distinction a measured property of the system rather than a labelling convention, and a defensible axis for the H3 generalization split.

    Real headroom for an adaptation method to demonstrate an effect.

    Project B · Spacecraft dynamics

    Oberth Efficiency Atlas

    A real burn is not instantaneous, so it retains only part of the textbook Oberth benefit. This project measures how much across six bodies and five engine classes, then tests whether one dimensionless number organizes the result.

    Finite-burn loss η 0.532 loss 12.7% of Δv

    burncoastunpowered arrival, impulsive departure

    Π 10.0

    keeps 53.2% of the impulsive Oberth bonus · loss 12.7% of Δv

    The Oberth effect means that the same Δv adds more energy when it is spent deep in a gravity well, at high speed. Mission design often treats that burn as instantaneous at periapsis. This project measures the correction. η is the share of the impulsive Oberth bonus — the gain in excess speed beyond Δv itself — retained by a real burn, and Π compares the burn duration with the time required to travel one periapsis distance at periapsis speed.

    The view above is a simulator, not a recording of a result. Moving the slider integrates the full flyby again with the same Δv distributed across a longer or shorter burn. The bright arc marks the burn and the blue line marks coast; dashed lines show the unpowered arrival and textbook impulsive departure. η and loss come directly from the simulator output.

    Held fixed: arrival speed equal to the escape speed at periapsis, Δv = 0.1 vp, Δv/c = 0.3, prograde thrust centred on periapsis, a point-mass planet. An illustrative case, not a mission. Π is the burn's duration in units of rp/vp.

    What the sweep shows.

    01 — Short burns

    Loss scales quadratically with Π.

    For a prograde burn centred on periapsis, the loss is at most about Π²/96 of Δv, for Π up to about 1 and modest Δv/vp and mass ratio; larger values scale it by a closed-form factor. A burn shorter than rp/vp (about 16% of a circular orbit's period at rp) therefore loses roughly 1% of its Δv or less. Across the 20,860 prograde sweep runs with Π ≤ 1, none exceeds 1.010 times the closed-form bound for its own Δv/vp and Δv/c.

    It is a leading-order bound, not a strict inequality, and it is probably close to a rule of thumb practitioners already use. It is reported as a clean statement for flybys, not as a discovery.

    02 — Long burns

    Half the bonus is gone at Π of about 8–40.

    The energy-based efficiency falls to one half at Π between 8.4 and 40 across all 1,143 engine and arrival families in the sweep, with a median of 26. The sweep is dimensionless, so this holds at every body: a burn longer than 10–40 periapsis timescales forfeits about half the Oberth bonus.

    Beyond the quadratic regime, slow arrivals (v∞/vesc ≲ 0.5) follow a Π−1/3 law until a crossover set by the arrival speed, then ln Π / Π; faster arrivals have no such middle regime. That is why the Sun, Jupiter and Saturn, with low arrival speeds, keep far more at a given Π.

    03 — One number, nearly

    Π alone does not collapse the data.

    At small Π, log(1−η) scatters by 0.2–0.3 dex against Π. Against Π√C, with C the closed-form prefactor from the theory, it scatters by about 0.001 dex, some 200 times tighter. The scatter that remains after collapsing on Π alone is mostly the arrival speed: v∞/vesc explains 73–92% of it for prograde burns, and the mass ratio is irrelevant to the collapse.

    04 — Real engines

    Retiming the burn rarely changes much.

    Optimising where the burn sits relative to periapsis recovers a median of 7×10−6 percentage points of η for chemical engines, 8×10−5 for nuclear thermal and 0.0068 for electric, over 5,895 valid mission samples. The largest single case is 4.1 points (nuclear thermal), and every gain above 0.1 points comes from starting the burn later than centred. A chemical burn centred on periapsis is within about 0.1 points of the optimum of the prograde family.

    One solar burn, three engine classes.

    Hibberd, Eubanks and Hein's solar Oberth burn to catch 3I/ATLAS spends 8.36 km/s at 3.2 solar radii. Flown with their motors, the impulsive model holds. Flown with other engines at the same geometry, it does not.

    The loss reaches 1% of Δv at Π of about 1. For the solid stack that means cutting the thrust by about 30; a single nuclear-thermal stage crosses it at an initial acceleration of roughly 0.83–0.88 m/s².

    The rebuild audit found problems before it passed. Committed outputs had gone stale after later code changes (Phase 1 files, one numbers file and the Phase 3 optimisation campaign), and an animation's first frame kept a marker from the still drawn before it. Each was regenerated or fixed, with a regression test for the bug, and none of the numbers quoted in the paper outline changed.

    What is not yet established.

    Tracked as carefully as the code, because the distinction is the point.

    • The novelty is provisional. The literature check was a web search, not a database search, and the 1966 paper by Robbins that the small-Π result is compared with could not be accessed, so claims that go beyond his scaling are provisional. The repository's claim register lists nine claims as provisional or assumption-dependent, each with what would settle it.
    • Point-mass gravity. Planet oblateness is neglected. A check on ten Jupiter and Saturn cases at 1.1 radii changes η by at most 0.0026, but absolute outgoing speeds shift by 21–28 m/s, so absolute results stay point-mass.
    • The planet-centred frame has limits. It stops being valid for a burn that starts outside the planet's sphere of influence. Those mission samples are masked, not given a number.
    • Representative presets, limited steering. The mission atlas uses representative presets, not hardware data, and only smooth pitch laws and one piecewise law were tested, so the optima hold within those families.
    • One earlier result is only partly reproduced. The model reproduces a large overestimate reported in a thesis by Confraria (2020) at a thrust-to-weight of 0.1, but not its exact size at higher thrust. The raw data behind that figure were not pursued.
    • The solar case is a simplified model. It is planar and point-mass, with constant thrust per motor, bracketed by two-level profiles. The unknown coast between stages is bracketed at 10 s and 60 s, and thermal limits are not modelled.

    Not yet a paper. The repository holds a working outline, the figures and the numbers behind it, and has been in feature freeze since 6 October. The only planned follow-up is the check against Robbins (1966).
